Vallisneri–Stancari
Correspondence

A digital scholarly edition of 30 letters (1707–1709) from Antonio Vallisneri to Vittorio Francesco Stancari. A corpus of early eighteenth-century Italian history of science, extracted and annotated using knowledge graph techniques and artificial intelligence.

30

The corpus

Thirty letters. One scientific correspondence. A window onto the Republic of Letters.

The letters are preserved in the Historical Archive of the University of Bologna, as part of a small collection acquired on the antiquarian market in 2013. Dating from 1705 to 1708, all thirty are addressed by Antonio Vallisneri to Francesco Vittorio Stancari — a young lecturer in mathematics, physicist, astronomer, and the first secretary of the newly founded Academy of Sciences of Bologna. Despite its modest size, the collection touches on many of the subjects that would remain central to Vallisneri's work for years to come, and offers a window onto the wider scientific network of the Republic of Letters.

01

Research themes

Across the letters, Vallisneri returns again and again to a handful of subjects: insects (especially the rose-gall fly), the seed of the water lentil, the ovary of the eel, chameleons, the ox brain, and the lucimeter — a French-invented instrument for measuring light, whose translation, construction and delivery to Bologna he tracks letter by letter between February and July 1707.

02

Writing & circulating knowledge

Ten of the thirty letters carry attachments: extracts from the journal La Galleria di Minerva, to which Vallisneri contributed for over twenty years (often under pseudonyms or students' names); pen-and-ink drawings of the lentil seed, the flea, the rose-gall fly and the eel's ovary; and, in one case, an actual specimen — three flies preserved inside a small folded paper packet.

03

Vallisneri's museum

From 1695 Vallisneri assembled a personal natural history museum, modelled on that of Ferdinando Marsili, acquiring specimens through fieldwork, exchange, gift and purchase — a whale jaw, an American serpent, a shark's head, and Indian seeds all entered his gallery via the correspondence documented here.

04

A wider network

Behind the exchange with Stancari lies a much larger web of correspondents — Marsili, Eustachio Manfredi, Giovanni Battista Morgagni, and the self-taught apothecary Diacinto Cestoni in Livorno, through whom Vallisneri obtained rare specimens such as the chameleons discussed throughout these letters. Of Vallisneri's roughly 12,000 surviving letters, more than ninety percent of his correspondents were based in northern Italy.

The correspondents

Two scholars, one exchange
S
Francesco Vittorio Stancari
Bologna, 1678 – Bologna, 1709

At the time of this correspondence (1705–1708), Stancari was a young lecturer in mathematics at the University of Bologna, as well as a physicist, astronomer, and the first secretary of the newly established Academy of Sciences of Bologna. The thirty letters Vallisneri addressed to him — acquired by the University's Historical Archive on the antiquarian market in 2013 — are the only surviving trace of their exchange, yet they touch on many of the subjects that would occupy Vallisneri for years to come.

V
Antonio Vallisneri
Trassilico, 1661 – Padua, 1730

Born in Trassilico and raised in Scandiano, Vallisneri travelled to Bologna in 1682 to study medicine under Marcello Malpighi, whose Galilean experimental philosophy shaped his entire career. After taking his degree in Reggio Emilia (1685) and practising medicine in Venice and Parma, he turned increasingly to natural history — first to entomology, refuting the doctrine of spontaneous generation. His 1696 Primo dialogo earned him, in 1700, the Chair of Practical Medicine at Padua, and in 1711 the more prestigious Chair of Theoretical Medicine, which he held until his death in 1730.
